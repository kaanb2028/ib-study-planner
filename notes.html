Unit 1 — Algebra and Function Basics AA HL

1. Lines, gradients and systems

Know the main forms of a straight-line equation, how gradient and intercepts are read, and how parallel/perpendicular relationships work.

y = mx + c   |   parallel: m₁ = m₂   |   perpendicular: m₁m₂ = −1
  • Solve systems of linear equations analytically and graphically.
  • Interpret an intersection as a simultaneous solution.

2. Functions: domain, range and notation

A function maps each allowed input to exactly one output. You need to identify domain, range, graph and function notation correctly.

  • Recognise different function types.
  • Use composite functions such as f(g(x)).
  • Find inverse functions when the function is one-to-one or suitably restricted.
  • Understand self-inverse functions.

3. Rational, radical and absolute-value functions

Focus on domains, excluded values, asymptotes and the effect of absolute value on graphs.

IB focus: be able to move between algebraic rules and graph behaviour, not just manipulate formulas.

4. Quadratic functions

Know standard, factorised and vertex forms, and use them to identify roots, axis of symmetry, intercepts and vertex.

ax² + bx + c = 0   |   x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac))/(2a)
  • Discriminant: b²−4ac determines the nature of roots.
  • Use sum/product of roots when appropriate.
  • Solve quadratic equations and inequalities analytically and graphically.

5. Graph transformations

Recognise translations, stretches/compressions and reflections, including composite transformations.

  • Special attention: y = |f(x)|, y = f(|x|) and reciprocal-type graph behaviour.
Unit 2 — Polynomial Functions

1. Polynomial structure and graphs

A polynomial has non-negative integer powers of x. Degree, leading coefficient and roots determine much of its graph.

  • Even-degree power graphs have broadly U-shaped end behaviour; odd-degree power graphs have broadly S-shaped end behaviour.
  • Identify constant, linear, quadratic and cubic forms.

2. Zeros, roots and factors

If P(a)=0, then x−a is a factor. Connect x-intercepts on the graph with algebraic roots.

Factor theorem: P(a)=0 ⇔ (x−a) is a factor

3. Remainder theorem and polynomial equations

Use substitution to obtain remainders and factorisation to solve higher-degree equations and inequalities.

Remainder when dividing by (x−a) = P(a)

4. Sum and product of roots

Use relationships between coefficients and roots to solve problems without always finding each root directly.

5. Complex numbers

The unit includes complex numbers as part of the toolkit for polynomial equations and roots.

Unit 3 — Sequences and Series

1. Arithmetic sequences

Each term changes by a constant common difference d.

uₙ = a + (n−1)d

Arithmetic sequences can be interpreted as linear models because each increase of 1 in n changes the term by d.

2. Geometric sequences and series

Each term is obtained by multiplying by a constant common ratio r.

uₙ = arⁿ⁻¹   |   Sₙ = a(1−rⁿ)/(1−r)

For an infinite geometric series, a finite sum exists only when |r| < 1.

S∞ = a/(1−r), for |r| < 1

3. Sigma notation

Use Σ notation to represent and manipulate sums efficiently.

4. Binomial theorem

Expand powers using binomial coefficients and identify individual terms. The material also includes validity of certain series expansions.

5. Counting principles

Work with permutations and combinations as part of the unit.

Unit 4 — Exponents and Logarithms

1. Exponential functions

The basic form is y=aˣ where a>0 and a≠1. The graph approaches y=0 but does not reach it, so y=0 is a horizontal asymptote.

  • Understand exponential growth and decay.
  • Connect exponential models to applications.
  • Understand the importance of e.

2. Logarithmic functions

Logarithms are inverses of exponential functions. Use logarithm laws to simplify expressions and solve equations.

logₐ(xy)=logₐx+logₐy   |   logₐ(x/y)=logₐx−logₐy   |   logₐ(xᵏ)=klogₐx

3. Solving exponential and logarithmic equations

Use equivalent bases where possible; otherwise apply logarithms and check domain restrictions for logarithmic expressions.

A.1 — Kinematics HL & SL

1. One-dimensional motion

Kinematics describes motion without explaining the forces causing it. Your material covers displacement, velocity, graphs, relative motion and SUVAT equations.

2. Scalars and vectors

  • Distance: total path length; scalar.
  • Displacement: change in position from start to finish; vector.
  • Speed: distance/time; scalar.
  • Velocity: displacement/time; vector.

3. Motion graphs

Interpret gradients and areas rather than memorising shapes.

  • Gradient of displacement–time graph → velocity.
  • Gradient of velocity–time graph → acceleration.
  • Area under velocity–time graph → displacement.
IB focus: a curved graph requires tangent/secant reasoning for instantaneous vs average rates.

4. SUVAT variables

s = displacement, u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = acceleration, t = time

SUVAT is valid only for constant (uniform) acceleration.

5. SUVAT equations

v = u + at
s = ((u+v)/2)t
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as

Each equation omits one variable. The method in your notes is: list known values, identify the unknown, then select the equation that excludes the unused variable.

Unit 1 — Introduction to Economics

1. Nature of economics

Economics studies how societies use scarce resources to produce goods and services, satisfy unlimited needs and wants, and distribute output among groups.

2. Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

  • Microeconomics: individual consumers, firms, choices and market interactions.
  • Macroeconomics: the economy as a whole using aggregates such as total output, employment and overall price level.

3. Scarcity, choice and sustainability

Scarcity exists because resources are finite while wants are unlimited. Scarcity forces choice, and every choice creates an opportunity cost. Sustainability concerns preserving the ability of the economy and environment to satisfy needs and wants in the future.

4. Factors of production

  • Land
  • Labour
  • Capital — including physical, human, natural and financial capital in your slides
  • Entrepreneurship

5. Opportunity cost

The value/benefit of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.

6. Three basic economic questions

  • What/how much to produce?
  • How to produce?
  • For whom to produce?

7. Economic systems

Compare free market, planned and mixed market systems through ownership, decision-making and rationing mechanisms.

8. Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

The PPC shows maximum combinations of two goods that can be produced with given resources and technology when resources are fully employed and efficiently used.

  • Inside PPC: unemployment/inefficiency.
  • On PPC: productive efficiency.
  • Outside PPC: currently unattainable.
  • Moving along PPC demonstrates opportunity cost.
  • Outward shift: growth in production possibilities.
Important distinction: actual growth can mean moving closer to the existing PPC; growth in production possibilities shifts the PPC outward.

9. Positive vs normative economics

Positive statements are testable/descriptive claims; normative statements contain value judgments about what ought to happen.

Unit 2 — Microeconomics: Competitive Markets

1. Demand

Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices over a given period.

  • Law of demand: price ↑ → quantity demanded ↓, ceteris paribus.
  • A change in own price causes movement along the demand curve.
  • A non-price determinant causes a shift of the demand curve.

2. Supply

Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to offer at different prices over a given period.

  • A change in own price creates movement along supply.
  • Non-price determinants shift the whole supply curve.

3. Market equilibrium and disequilibrium

Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At the old price after a curve shift, excess demand or excess supply creates price signals that push the market toward a new equilibrium.

4. Changes in demand and supply

Your slides emphasise reading the chain of adjustment:

curve shifts → shortage/surplus at old price → price signal → movements along curves → new equilibrium

5. Price mechanism: signals, incentives and resource allocation

Prices transmit information and create incentives. Higher prices can signal scarcity and encourage producers to expand output; lower prices can signal excess supply and discourage production.

6. Allocative efficiency

Demand = Marginal Benefit (MB)
Supply = Marginal Cost (MC)
Allocative efficiency: MB = MC

At allocative efficiency, society produces the quantity where the marginal benefit from the last unit equals its marginal cost.

7. Consumer, producer and community surplus

  • Consumer surplus: willingness to pay minus price actually paid.
  • Producer surplus: price received minus minimum price producers were willing to accept.
  • Community surplus: consumer surplus + producer surplus.

The HL material includes calculation of these areas using triangles and trapezia.

Unit 1.1 — What is a Business?

1. Nature of business

A business is a decision-making organization that uses inputs to produce goods and/or provide services.

Inputs → Processes → Outputs
  • Inputs: land, labour, capital, enterprise.
  • Processes: HR, finance, marketing, operations management.
  • Outputs: goods and services.

2. Functional areas

Human resources, finance, operations management and marketing are interdependent; they must coordinate to achieve organizational goals.

3. Sectors of industry

  • Primary: extraction/raw materials.
  • Secondary: manufacturing/conversion into finished products.
  • Tertiary: services.
  • Quaternary: knowledge creation/distribution.

The chain of production links extraction → manufacturing → services → consumer.

4. Added value

Your notes define added value as the difference between the final selling price and the original/input cost.

5. Customer vs consumer

Customer: buys the product. Consumer: uses the product.

6. Entrepreneurship

An entrepreneur plans, organizes and manages a business while taking financial risk. Your notes stress leadership, risk-taking, innovation, ambition, creativity, communication and self-motivation.

Unit 1.2 — Types of Business Entities

1. Private vs public sector

Private-sector organizations are owned and controlled by private individuals/businesses. Public-sector organizations are owned/controlled by government and often provide essential services.

2. Sole traders

One owner, usually easy and inexpensive to establish, but generally unincorporated with unlimited liability.

3. Partnerships

Two or more owners share finance, responsibilities and profits/losses. A deed of partnership can define roles, contributions and distribution of profit/loss.

4. Privately vs publicly held companies

Companies are incorporated and have a separate legal identity. Shareholders benefit from limited liability. A privately held company's shares are not traded publicly; a publicly held company can sell shares on a public stock exchange.

5. Social enterprises and NGOs

Social enterprises place social objectives at the core of operations while still generating revenue. Your materials include private/public sector social enterprises, cooperatives and non-profit NGOs.

Unit 1.3 — Business Objectives

1. Vision statement

A vision statement describes the organization's long-term aspiration — what it wants to become in the distant future. It gives direction and helps unify decisions and culture.

2. Mission statement

A mission statement expresses the organization's underlying purpose and core values — what the business does and why it exists. It is generally more immediate than a vision and is qualitative rather than a timed numerical target.

3. Vision vs mission

  • Vision: “What do we want to become?”
  • Mission: “What is our business / why do we exist?”
  • Vision is very long-term; mission is more connected to current purpose and daily guiding values.

4. Common business objectives

Your Unit 1.3 specification includes growth, profit, protecting shareholder value and ethical objectives.

5. Strategic vs tactical objectives

Strategic objectives are longer-term, organization-wide directions. Tactical objectives are shorter-term, more specific actions used to implement strategy.

IB application: in case studies, classify the level of an objective and then judge whether the tactic actually supports the firm's mission and strategy.

6. Corporate social responsibility (CSR)

CSR is included in your Unit 1.3 content and links business objectives to responsibilities toward stakeholders and society.
